Every other week in this track teaches you to build something. This one teaches you what to build, for whom, and how to prove it worked. A forward-deployed role is the same engineering plus a customer: someone has a vague problem, a regulated environment and a deadline, and you are the person in the room who has to turn that into a system they will sign off on. The code here is deliberately tiny, because the skill is in the questions, not the syntax.

Concept 1 — A Request Is a Symptom, Not a Spec

"We want an AI assistant for claims" is not a requirement. It names a technology and hides the problem. Discovery is the work of converting it into six answers you can write down: which system holds the data, who owns it, how much of it there is per day, whether it is regulated, what "better" means in a number, and who can say yes.

A useful habit is to keep the answers in a structure rather than in your head, so that a missing answer is visible instead of forgotten. The check below is trivial on purpose: the discipline is that you run it after every customer call.

Example:

REQUIRED = ["system_of_record", "data_owner", "volume_per_day",
            "regulated_data", "success_metric", "decision_maker"]
VAGUE = {"faster", "better", "cheaper", "smarter"}

call_notes = {
    "system_of_record": "ClaimCenter (on-prem), notes exported nightly to MSSQL",
    "volume_per_day": 1800,
    "regulated_data": "yes - health information in some claims",
    "success_metric": "faster",
}

def discovery_gaps(notes):
    missing = [f for f in REQUIRED if f not in notes]
    vague = [k for k, v in notes.items()
             if isinstance(v, str) and v.strip().lower() in VAGUE]
    return missing, vague

missing, vague = discovery_gaps(call_notes)
print("missing:", missing)
print("too vague:", vague)

Genuine output:

too vague: ['success_metric']

Exercise:
Add a seventh required field you would insist on before writing any code for a claims
assistant, and justify it in two sentences. Then rewrite the vague success_metric as a number
with a unit, a baseline and a time window.


Starting point: The call_notes dictionary and REQUIRED list from this concept's Example.


Expected output: a defensible extra field (for example retention_rules or human_review_step)
and a metric of the form "median handling time from 18 minutes to under 12, measured over the
first 4 weeks of a pilot", not "faster".




Concept 2 — Map the Data Before You Promise Anything


In regulated customers the data, not the model, decides the schedule. A source with no named
owner cannot be accessed at all, and a source whose access request takes six weeks sets your
critical path no matter how good your retrieval is. Do this inventory in the first meeting, and
do it with the customer, because the owner column is usually the part nobody has written down.


Example:


sources = [
    {"name": "claim_notes",      "owner": "Claims Ops",     "access_days": 10, "pii": True},
    {"name": "policy_master",    "owner": None,             "access_days": None, "pii": True},
    {"name": "call_transcripts", "owner": "Contact Centre", "access_days": 45, "pii": True},
]

def blockers(sources, budget_days=14):
    found = []
    for s in sources:
        if s["owner"] is None:
            found.append((s["name"], "no owner"))
        elif s["access_days"] > budget_days:
            found.append((s["name"], f"access takes {s['access_days']}d > {budget_days}d"))
    return found

for name, why in blockers(sources):
    print(name, "-", why)

Genuine output:


policy_master - no owner
call_transcripts - access takes 45d > 14d

Exercise:
Using the same list, decide which single source you would build the first prototype on, and
state what you would tell the customer about the other two. Name the one fact that would change
your mind.


Starting point: The sources list and the blockers result from this concept's Example.


Expected output: claim_notes as the prototype source (the only one with an owner and an
access time inside the budget), a plain statement that the other two are scheduled behind their
access requests rather than dropped, and a changing-your-mind fact such as the policy-master
owner being found this week.




Concept 3 — Who Has to Say Yes


The person who asked for the project is rarely the only person who can stop it. In a bank or
insurer, security, compliance and model risk each hold a veto that arrives late and costs
weeks. The cheap fix is to list them on day one and get each one into a conversation before you
have something to demo, so that the demo is not their first sight of it.


Example:


required_signoffs = {"business_owner", "security", "compliance", "model_risk"}
engaged = {"business_owner", "security"}

print("not yet engaged:", sorted(required_signoffs - engaged))

Genuine output:


not yet engaged: ['compliance', 'model_risk']

Exercise:
Write the one-sentence ask you would send to each unengaged sign-off to book a first, short
conversation. Each ask must say what you need from them and by when, and must not require them to
understand the technology.


Starting point: The required_signoffs and engaged sets from this concept's Example.


Expected output: two short asks, one for compliance and one for model risk, each naming a
concrete input (for example "which of these data fields may not leave the EU region") and a date.




BFSI Reality Check — Day 1


Legacy angle: The honest answer to "where does the data live" is often three places: the
system of record, a nightly export nobody owns, and a spreadsheet someone fixes by hand. Ask
which of the three the business actually trusts, because that is the one your numbers will be
judged against.


Compliance angle: Model risk management guidance such as the US Federal Reserve and OCC's
SR 11-7 expects a documented purpose, data lineage and an owner for any model used in a
decision. A discovery log that records the owner, the data and the intended use is the first page
of that documentation, written for free.


What breaks in production: The project is scoped against the data the sponsor described,
and the data the system actually receives is different: another encoding, another cut-off time, a
second source for the same field. Record in discovery who confirmed each source, not just that it
exists.

Concept 1 — Measure the Human Baseline First


An AI system is "good" only relative to what it replaces. If nobody measured how long the work
takes today, any improvement you claim is unfalsifiable and any regression is deniable. Take a
small sample of real, current cases before building anything, and report a median and a high
percentile, not a mean, because a few very slow cases dominate the mean.


Example:


import statistics

human_minutes = [14, 22, 9, 31, 17, 12, 26, 19, 15, 41]
ordered = sorted(human_minutes)
p90 = ordered[-(-9 * len(ordered) // 10) - 1]   # nearest-rank, 90th percentile

print("median:", statistics.median(human_minutes))
print("p90:", p90)
print("mean:", statistics.mean(human_minutes))

mean: 20.6

Explain in three sentences why a customer who is told "the assistant averages 15 minutes" might

Starting point: The human_minutes sample and the three printed statistics from this concept's Example.


Expected output: an explanation that the mean is pulled down by the many easy cases, that the
painful cases sit in the tail (p90 of 31 minutes), and that the comparison must be made on the
same percentile as the customer's complaint.




Concept 2 — Acceptance Criteria Are a Test Suite You Agree on Early


Agree the pass mark before the first result exists. Once numbers are on the table every party
negotiates in their own favour. Write the thresholds down as data, run the measurement against
them, and let the output be a decision, not an opinion.


Example:


criteria = {"accuracy_min": 0.90, "p95_latency_s_max": 4.0, "pii_leaks_max": 0}
measured = {"accuracy": 0.93, "p95_latency_s": 5.2, "pii_leaks": 0}

checks = {
    "accuracy":      measured["accuracy"] >= criteria["accuracy_min"],
    "p95_latency_s": measured["p95_latency_s"] <= criteria["p95_latency_s_max"],
    "pii_leaks":     measured["pii_leaks"] <= criteria["pii_leaks_max"],
}
for name, ok in checks.items():
    print(f"{name:14s} {'PASS' if ok else 'FAIL'}")
print("decision:", "go" if all(checks.values()) else "no-go")

Concept 3 — Not All Errors Cost the Same


Accuracy hides the thing the business cares about, which is money and harm. A missed fraud flag
and a wrongly blocked payment are both "errors" and they are not the same size. Put an assumed
cost on each, compare candidate systems by expected cost, and the right model is often not the
most accurate one. The figures below are illustrative assumptions, not benchmarks.


Example:


COST_MISSED_FRAUD = 250.0   # assumed loss per fraud case that gets through
COST_FALSE_ALARM = 8.0      # assumed review cost per wrongly flagged case

def expected_cost(missed, false_alarms):
    return missed * COST_MISSED_FRAUD + false_alarms * COST_FALSE_ALARM

model_a = expected_cost(missed=4, false_alarms=120)
model_b = expected_cost(missed=9, false_alarms=40)
print(model_a, model_b)
print("cheaper:", "A" if model_a < model_b else "B")

Editable. Blocks on a page share one session, so run them in order.
Genuine output:


1960.0 2570.0
cheaper: A

Exercise:
Find the false-alarm cost above which model B becomes the cheaper system, holding everything
else constant, and state in one sentence why the customer, not you, must supply that number.


Starting point: The two models' counts and COST_MISSED_FRAUD from this concept's Example.


Expected output: a break-even false-alarm cost of about 15.63 per case (solve
4*250 + 120*x = 9*250 + 40*x, so model B is cheaper above that figure), and the point that the cost of a review is an operational fact
only the customer can state.




BFSI Reality Check — Day 2


Legacy angle: The baseline often does not exist because the legacy system never recorded
handling time, only open and close dates that include weekends and queue waits. Say what the
number measures before you quote it.


Compliance angle: Under SR 11-7 style validation, the stated performance of a model has to be
reproducible by someone who did not build it. Thresholds agreed in writing, plus a fixed
measurement set, are what make a later validator's job possible.


What breaks in production: Acceptance is signed on a pilot sample, then the real mix of cases
shifts (a catastrophe event, a product launch) and the system is judged against the old numbers
without anyone re-measuring. Put a re-measurement date in the acceptance document.

Concept 1 — Cut the Scope Before the Customer Does


A first prototype has a fixed number of days and an unbounded wish list. Estimate each item with
its assumption, rank by value per day, and keep what fits. The surprise is usually that the
evaluation harness belongs in the first cut: without it the prototype cannot prove anything,
which means it is a demo and not a pilot.


Example:


tasks = [  # (name, days, value 1-5)
    ("ingest claim notes", 2, 5),
    ("retrieval + citations", 3, 5),
    ("eval harness", 2, 4),
    ("admin UI", 4, 1),
    ("SSO integration", 3, 2),
]
budget = 8
chosen, used = [], 0
for name, days, value in sorted(tasks, key=lambda t: t[2] / t[1], reverse=True):
    if used + days <= budget:
        chosen.append(name)
        used += days
print(chosen, used)

Concept 2 — Pick the Smallest Technique That Solves It


Customers ask for fine-tuning and agents because they have heard the words. The order that
usually wins is the reverse: prompting first, retrieval when the answer lives in private or
changing documents, an agent when the system must take actions, and fine-tuning when you need a
consistent style or format and have labelled examples. Encode the order so the argument is about
the inputs, not about taste.


Example:


def suggest(needs_private_or_fresh_data, needs_actions,
            needs_style_or_format, has_labelled_examples):
    steps = ["prompting"]
    if needs_private_or_fresh_data:
        steps.append("retrieval (RAG)")
    if needs_actions:
        steps.append("agent with tools, only after retrieval works")
    if needs_style_or_format and has_labelled_examples:
        steps.append("fine-tuning")
    return steps

print(suggest(True, False, False, False))
print(suggest(True, True, True, False))

Genuine output:


['prompting', 'retrieval (RAG)']
['prompting', 'retrieval (RAG)', 'agent with tools, only after retrieval works']

Concept 3 — Put the Trade-Off in One Table an Executive Can Read


Quality, latency and cost trade against each other, and an executive decides on cost first. Give
a monthly estimate with the assumptions printed next to it, so the number can be challenged on its
inputs. The prices below are assumptions for illustration, not a quote from any provider.


Example:


queries_per_day = 2000
input_tokens, output_tokens = 3000, 400
price_in_per_m, price_out_per_m = 0.50, 1.50   # assumed USD per million tokens
days = 30

cost_in = queries_per_day * input_tokens * days / 1_000_000 * price_in_per_m
cost_out = queries_per_day * output_tokens * days / 1_000_000 * price_out_per_m
print(f"input ${cost_in:.2f} + output ${cost_out:.2f} = ${cost_in + cost_out:.2f} per month")

Genuine output:

BFSI Reality Check — Day 3


Legacy angle: The fastest path to a working demo is usually a vendor API and a cloud index,
and the customer's approved-vendor list and data-residency rules usually rule both out. Ask for
the approved list in discovery, not after you have built on something off it.


Compliance angle: Under the EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act and similar third-party
risk regimes, a financial entity has to manage the risk of its ICT providers. A new model
provider is a new third party, with its own review, so "just call the API" is a procurement
project.


What breaks in production: The scope that was cut to fit the prototype becomes the production
scope by default, because nobody wrote down what was deferred. Keep a visible "not built yet"
list and hand it over with the system.




Day 4 — Prove It: Evidence the Customer Will Sign Off On

Concept 1 — A Number Without an Interval Is a Guess


"It is 90 percent accurate" means something different on 50 examples and on 1000. With a small
golden set the honest range is wide, and a customer who later measures 82 percent will feel
misled even though you were not wrong. Report the interval. The Wilson score interval behaves
sensibly at small sample sizes, unlike the naive plus-or-minus.


Example:


import math

def wilson(successes, n, z=1.96):
    p = successes / n
    denom = 1 + z * z / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / denom
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / denom
    return centre - half, centre + half

for n in (50, 200, 1000):
    lo, hi = wilson(round(0.90 * n), n)
    print(f"n={n:<5d} accuracy 0.90  95% interval {lo:.3f} to {hi:.3f}")

n=200   accuracy 0.90  95% interval 0.851 to 0.934
n=1000  accuracy 0.90  95% interval 0.880 to 0.917

Concept 2 — Read the Failures, Do Not Just Count Them


Two systems can both score 90 percent while failing in completely different places. Take the
wrong answers, label each with a cause, and count the causes. The most common cause tells you what
to fix next and, just as usefully, what to tell the customer you are not going to fix.


Example:


from collections import Counter

failures = [
    "retrieved wrong policy version", "retrieved wrong policy version",
    "missed exclusion clause", "retrieved wrong policy version",
    "arithmetic error", "missed exclusion clause",
    "retrieved wrong policy version", "outdated document",
]
for cause, count in Counter(failures).most_common():
    print(f"{count}  {cause}")

Concept 3 — Decide Go or No-Go Before You See the Result


Write the go/no-go rule down before the pilot ends, using the lower bound of the interval, not the
point estimate. It protects you from wishful reading and it protects the customer from a result
that was shaped to the decision.


Example:


def decide(lower_bound, bar=0.85, harmful_errors=0):
    if harmful_errors > 0:
        return "no-go: harmful errors observed"
    return "go" if lower_bound >= bar else "no-go: not proven above the bar"

print(decide(lower_bound=0.862))
print(decide(lower_bound=0.862, harmful_errors=1))
print(decide(lower_bound=0.81))

Editable. Blocks on a page share one session, so run them in order.
Genuine output:


go
no-go: harmful errors observed
no-go: not proven above the bar

Exercise:
Define what counts as a "harmful error" for a claims assistant in one sentence, so that the rule
above can be applied by someone who was not in the room.


Starting point: The decide function and its three calls from this concept's Example.


Expected output: a definition tied to a customer-visible consequence, for example "a payout
amount or coverage statement that is wrong in the customer's favour or against it by more than
the agreed tolerance", not a general word like "bad".




BFSI Reality Check — Day 4


Legacy angle: The golden set is built from real cases, and real cases contain customer data
that cannot leave the controlled environment. Plan the labelling and storage inside the customer's
perimeter from the start, because exporting it later is a security review of its own.


Compliance angle: For anything an organisation classifies as high-risk under the EU AI Act,
documented accuracy, human oversight and logging are expected. A pilot report that states the
measured interval, the failure causes and the go/no-go rule is the draft of that evidence.


What breaks in production: The golden set quietly goes stale: the product changes, the labelled
answers no longer match current policy, and the system is graded against yesterday's truth. Give the
set an owner and a refresh date.

Concept 1 — The Handover Test: Can They Run It Without You


A deployment that only its builder can operate has not been delivered, it has been rented. Before
you leave, list what the customer team must be able to do alone, and test each item by having them
do it while you watch and say nothing.


Example:


capabilities = {
    "redeploy after a config change": True,
    "rotate the API key": True,
    "add a document source": False,
    "read an alert and know what to do": False,
    "re-run the golden-set evaluation": True,
}
gaps = [name for name, can in capabilities.items() if not can]
print(f"{len(capabilities) - len(gaps)} of {len(capabilities)} handed over")
for g in gaps:
    print("still needs you:", g)

next customer does not hit the same wall. A field report is useful when it is structured, specific

Concept 3 — The FDE Interview Has Three Parts


Interview loops for this role tend to test three things: building a working feature with an AI
coding agent, designing an AI system for a stated customer problem with trade-offs on quality,
latency, cost and security, and handling a vague customer scenario by asking questions, defining
success and explaining a choice simply. Everything above is preparation for the third. Score
yourself against a rubric, with the weights written down in advance.


Example:


weights = {"asked clarifying questions": 3, "defined success as a number": 3,
           "named the trade-off": 2, "explained it without jargon": 2}
my_session = {"asked clarifying questions": 3, "defined success as a number": 1,
              "named the trade-off": 2, "explained it without jargon": 1}

total = sum(weights.values())
got = sum(my_session.values())
weakest = min(my_session, key=lambda k: my_session[k] / weights[k])
print(f"{got}/{total}  weakest area: {weakest}")

BFSI Reality Check — Day 5


Legacy angle: The customer team that inherits the system is often two people who also run
three other systems. A handover that assumes a dedicated team will not be operated; size the
runbook for the real staffing.


Compliance angle: Operational resilience rules expect a financial entity to be able to
explain who operates a system, how incidents are escalated and how it is recovered. The handover
checklist and runbook above are that explanation in practical form.


What breaks in production: The builder leaves, the one person who understood the export quirks
changes team, and the knowledge existed only in a chat thread. Put the quirks in the repository
next to the code.




Architecture Decision — Week 00


Choosing between: embedding with the customer from day one vs. building a prototype remotely
and bringing it to them.


Rule of thumb: embed first when the data landscape, the sign-offs or the definition of success
are unknown, because those are learned in rooms and not from documents, and a prototype built on a
wrong assumption costs more than the meetings. Build remotely first when the problem, data and bar
are already written down and the main uncertainty is technical, and then bring the working
prototype in early rather than late. Either way, fix the acceptance criteria and the go/no-go
rule in writing before the first result exists, because every decision after that point is
negotiated against whatever numbers are on the table.
